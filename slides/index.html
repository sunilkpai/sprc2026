[0:15] Motivation in one breath. The numbers on the left are the crisis: capex above three hundred billion a year across the hyperscalers on 2025 guidance, datacenters going from about four percent of US electricity in 2023 to a projected seven to twelve percent by 2028 in the Berkeley Lab report, and campuses now sized in gigawatts. In 2022 we built a chip that trains with light, on the premise that the multiply was the expensive thing. Since then the models reached what we now call AGI-class capability and the workload changed underneath us: transformers, four-bit inference, decode that waits on memory. The purpose of the talk is to stimulate a discussion about the role of photonic analog computing in AI, now that we understand where near-AGI LLM designs actually bottleneck: memory bandwidth in decode, precision in training, silicon area per weight, and the split between weight-stationary multiplies and attention. Redo the 2022 argument against that, and against the 2026 competition. I will give my own answers, and I would rather be argued with than agreed with.

The 2010s thesis: a programmable mesh for quantum and AI

An MZI: \(\phi\), coupler, \(\theta\), coupler. Sweep \(\theta,\phi\) and all the light goes to one port. A mesh of them is any \(N\times N\) unitary.

Quantum: the mesh is the computer
  • Linear optics, photons and detectors are universal (KLM 2001)
  • Reprogrammable 6-mode interferometer on a chip (Carolan 2015)
AI: the mesh as a matrix engine
  • Coherent nanophotonic deep learning (Shen 2017)
  • \(N^2\) MACs per pass for \(O(N)\) conversions

Well over $1B of venture funding followed the AI thesis, 2017 to 2023.

[1:00] One device, five literatures: universal linear optics after Reck and Clements, self-configuring optics after Miller, quantum photonics after Carolan, neural networks after Shen, programmable photonics after Bogaerts. The animation is the primitive: sweep the two phases of one MZI and steer all the light into one port; that is how a mesh is programmed and how it is read out. Two communities adopted the same hardware for opposite reasons. Quantum needs the mesh to be exact. AI needs it to be cheap. Keep that tension in mind.

[2:00] Do not land a conclusion here. Three places where the mesh is the signal path and there is no GPU to compare against: quantum, analog signal processing, and the edge, where the budget is milliwatts and a few ports. The figure is the self-correcting configuration: you send in the vector you want the row to be, and null each interferometer's output by feedback; every fabrication error is absorbed because you measure the device rather than model it. A balanced binary tree nulls its columns in parallel, log N steps instead of N minus one. It is the same power measurement that gives the gradient in the Science paper.

Evaluating photonic mesh on a transformer model

  • Transformer: a token (a word piece, a vector of length \(d\)) passes through \(80\) copies of the block on the left to predict the next token
  • MLP (green): two fixed weight matrices with a nonlinearity between them; the same weights for every token
  • Attention (amber): the token is compared with every earlier token, whose keys and values sit in the KV cache
[2:45] Definitions first, this is a photonics room. A token is a word piece; the model turns it into a vector of eight thousand numbers and pushes it through eighty copies of the block on the left. Green boxes are ordinary matrix-times-vector with a weight matrix that never changes during inference; that is the thing an optical mesh can hold. The amber box is attention: the new token looks back at every earlier token in the conversation, whose keys and values were computed earlier and are kept in memory, the KV cache. On the right, the two regimes. Prefill is reading the prompt: all its tokens go through at once, so each weight is fetched once and reused for every token, and the chip is busy multiplying. That is the mesh's case, and training looks the same with thousands of tokens per weight. Decode is writing the answer: one token per step, and every step reads every weight and the whole cache from memory to produce a single vector, about one multiply per byte fetched. No multiplier helps there, optical or not. Photonics was adopted in the datacenter for moving numbers between chips; the multiply stayed electronic.

What changed after 2022?

Precision collapsed
FP8 in 2022, FP4 in 2024. Inference walked into the regime analog optics always needed.
Attention moved the bottleneck
The MLP is compute-bound; attention in decode is bandwidth-bound. A faster multiply helps the first and not the second.
Inference market share is growing
ChatGPT, then test-time compute. Inference is a rising share of deployed FLOPs and spend; training is not shrinking, it is being outgrown.
ADC efficiency hasn't improved
Conversion energy per sample has barely moved while logic kept scaling, and every activation pays it twice.

Four conditions for optics to win: wide fan-in (still hard), low precision (now true), static weights (true for inference), data already analog (not in a datacenter).

[3:45] Two of the four conditions flipped in our favor since 2022. The two that did not are both about where the data lives. Hold that: it is why the verdict at the end is about prefill and training, not decode.

[4:30] Three ways to make light do a matrix. The mesh holds the weights as phases and does the multiply and the sum by interference, so it needs coherence across the whole array and one interferometer per weight. The crossbar, which is what Envise actually is, holds the weights as attenuation cells, broadcasts the input, and sums photocurrents on a wire, so there is no phase to hold but still a device per weight. Temporal mapping holds nothing: the weights arrive as pulses from memory and multiply the input at a detector, so the silicon is order N and the cost moves to memory bandwidth. All three convert at the edge, order N per N squared multiplies; that shared term is the energy story later. Say once that the nonlinearity stays electronic: optical nonlinearities, electro-optic, saturable, all-optical, are real and interesting, and the 2023 SM even sketches a low-loss distributed one, but they are a separate talk and none of the energy numbers here depend on them. What differs is where the weight lives, and that decides footprint, stability, training, and what happens in decode.

In situ backpropagation: the method

1 Forward. Send \(x\), record the tap powers \(p_\eta\).
2 Backward. Send the error \(y_{\mathrm{adj}}\) from the output; record \(p_{\eta,\mathrm{adj}}\).
3 Sum. Send \(x - i\,x_{\mathrm{adj}}^{*}\); the tap power is the gradient.
\[ \underbrace{\textcolor{#3A8FB7}{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\eta} = -\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(x_\eta\,x_{\mathrm{adj},\eta}\right)}}_{\textcolor{#3A8FB7}{\text{adjoint method}}} \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\left(p_{\eta,\mathrm{sum}} - p_\eta - p_{\eta,\mathrm{adj}}\right)}_{\textcolor{#5B6B7A}{\text{digital subtraction}}} \;=\; \underbrace{\textcolor{#D9821E}{\tfrac14\left(p_{\eta,+} - p_{\eta,-}\right)}}_{\textcolor{#D9821E}{\text{experimental implementation}}} \]

The gradient of every phase shifter is measured on the chip itself, so fabrication errors are included automatically and no model of the device is needed. Fig. 1E, Pai et al., Science 380, 398 (2023).

[5:30] Point at the three colored paths. Forward, backward, sum. The gradient is a power measurement: interference between the forward field and the back-propagated error at each phase shifter, exactly the adjoint method from inverse design, and it does not care about fabrication error because the chip is doing the differentiation. Third formula is the analog version: toggle the adjoint phase, high-pass filter, done.

All-analog gradient computation

  • A, B: 15 MZIs, TiN heaters, a tap at every shifter, an IR camera on a stage
  • C to F: a summing circuit with a phase toggle between the forward and backward fields; homodyne-and-integrate, but for gradients
  • \(1000\) iterations in \(31\) hours, dominated by microscope-stage motion
Fig. 2 and Fig. S1, Pai et al. (2023). Ring and moons trained on chip to \(90\) to \(98\%\).
[6:30] This is what a research demonstration looks like: the detector is a camera, the "ADC" is a microscope stage, and the phase shifters are heaters at 50 milliwatts each. Panels C to F are the part to dwell on: the analog update. Toggle the adjoint phase, the AC part of the tap power is the gradient, integrate it over the batch, write the voltage. That is the batch-gradient story on the energy slide, demonstrated on one MZI with op-amps. Every number in the energy model later replaces one of these with an integrated part. The point of the slide is that the protocol worked on a chip with real loss and real crosstalk.

What the paper showed, and what is new today

2023: Pai et al.
  • Backpropagation on an optical chip, for the first time
  • Gradients of every phase from three optical passes, of the device you have
  • An analog update: no digital readback of \(N^2\) parameters
  • Energy model: the ADC is the bottleneck, not photons (backup)
2026: what changed
  • Transformers: the mesh fits the MLP in prefill and training; decode is memory-bound
  • Inference went to 4 bits; training still needs bf16-class activations
  • Envise: \(128^2\) crossbar at \(1.2\,\mathrm{pJ/op}\); Jalapeño: MXFP4 at \(52\,\mathrm{fJ/op}\)
  • Mesh has poor footprint efficiency (\(10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mm}^2\) per weight), requires time-division multiplexing
Main message
Most optical-computing marketing misses the electrical bottleneck: the multiply is nearly free, but NOT the conversions. Innovations that reduce conversions per operation are what make these technologies worth the investment.
[8:15] Rough summary. The paper: the first backpropagation on an optical chip, every gradient in three passes measured on the device itself, an analog update that never reads the parameters back, and a model that already said conversions would dominate: the ADC is half the energy at 8 bits and the only clock in the system, and a 5 nanometre converter changes the clock, not the energy per op; the projection is in the backup. What is new: the workload is transformers, and the mesh fits the weight-stationary part in prefill and training while decode is a memory problem for everyone; inference went to four bits, which is the analog regime, while training still wants sixteen-bit-class activations; Lightmatter shipped a 128-wide crossbar and measured 1.2 picojoules per op, and OpenAI's inference ASIC does four-bit at 52 femtojoules, so the bar is high; and the mesh's real problem is area, one interferometer per weight, a hundred times more silicon than a transistor MAC. The main message, and the thing I would like argued with: most optical-computing marketing misses the electrical bottleneck. The multiply is nearly free in every one of these schemes, mesh, crossbar or temporal mapping; what is not free is the DAC, the ADC and the memory read around it, and those are the same parts a GPU uses. Envise measured a factor of forty between core and package. So the innovations worth funding are the ones that reduce conversions per operation: wider fan-in, batch-integrated readout, activations that stay optical between layers, weights that never move. Without those, the investment does not pay. The energy chart, the tradeoff table and my own proposal are in the backup if anyone wants them.

Does it scale? MNIST at \(N=64\) with hardware errors backup

  • Two \(64\)-port meshes on MNIST; batch \(600\), \(100\) epochs
  • Grid of \(72\) error settings: tap noise, loss, I/O amplitude and phase
  • Ideal \(97.2\%\) test accuracy; with errors, \(95\%\) on average
  • Tap photodiode noise (\(s_{\mathrm{tap}}\)) and phase readout error (\(\sigma_\phi\)) dominate; loss and I/O amplitude barely matter
  • Noisy gradients still converge, just later
Pai et al., Science 380, 398 (2023), Fig. 4.
Backup. The experiment was four ports; this is the scaling question. Two 64-port meshes on MNIST, simulated with the same in situ protocol and a grid of 72 error settings. The ideal network reaches 97 percent; the average with hardware error is 95. Only two error sources matter: noise on the tap photodiodes that measure the powers, and error in the phase readout. Loss and I/O amplitude error hardly move the curves. And the curves converge anyway, just a little later: analog gradient noise costs epochs, not accuracy.

Energy per operation: the 2023 model, Envise, and a 4-bit projection backup

Backup. All photonic bars are projected from the 2023 model; Envise is measured. Say it. Chart basis: Science 2023 SM Tables S1 to S4 at N equals 128 and 1 gigahertz. Envise: 78 watts plus 1.6 watts optical at 65.5 TOPS, 0.25 watts in the encode path including its weight DACs, from the Nature 2025 SI. Assumptions behind the 4-bit projection: ADC energy scales as 2 to the bits (Walden); the Nature SI's thermal-limited 4 to the bits would be 16 times more optimistic. Digital op divided by 3 is the B200 FP4 to H100 INT8 ratio at the wall. Optical power scales as 4 to the bits from shot noise, so 4-bit inference needs 256 times less light and 12-bit gradient readout needs 256 times more; that is the 3.4 picojoule per op bar, 3 picojoules of it laser. Training runs bf16 activations, and bf16 is an 8-bit significand with an 8-bit exponent; the per-vector normalisation in the 2023 protocol supplies a block exponent, so the analog path carries the 8-bit mantissa and needs an Envise-class 11-bit ADC for accumulation headroom, not a 16-bit converter. That is the 141 femtojoule bar: the ADC term goes from 22 to 110 and everything else stays. It is 1.3 times under Cerebras at FP16 and 5 times under an H100, and it loses per-element exponents, which is the honest caveat. The gradient readout is batch-integrated, which is what the SM's analog update does: the tap current is integrated over the batch and digitised once per phase shifter per batch, so a slow 12 to 16 bit ADC at f over M is enough and the average gains half of log2 M bits, six at M equals 4096. Realistic M today is thousands of tokens per weight per device for an LLM micro-batch and millions of positions per layer for a CNN, so the SM's 16 to 256 was conservative. Two ADCs, two rates: activations at 8 bits and 1 gigasample, gradients at 12 plus bits and megasamples. Segmented phase shifters everywhere means every weight cell needs b digital lines from the control die: 65 thousand contacts at 4 bits for a 128 mesh. Baselines: B200 FP8 at 220 and FP4 at 110 femtojoules per op, OpenAI's Jalapeno MXFP4 ASIC at 52, all peak over package power; Cerebras WSE-3 at 180 for FP16. Say the activation point: a mesh's weight precision is free per op, because a phase is held rather than re-encoded, so W4A8, the format Kimi K3 and DeepSeek actually run, costs the same as W8A8: 45 femtojoules, five times under B200 FP8. Only if the activations go to four bits does the ADC collapse and the bar fall to 14. The honest gap at four bits is under four times before any shell is counted. Three things to say. One: the 2023 tables already predicted that conversions would dominate, and Envise confirmed it with a factor of 40 between the tensor core and the package. Two: 4-bit inference is where the optics win on paper. Three: training gets a different gift, time: integrate the gradient over the batch and precision comes from averaging, not from light or from a fast ADC. Systematic tap errors do not average, so calibration is the price.

Backup. This is the slide to leave up. Read it top to bottom. Energy per op the mesh wins, by four times at four bits before its shell, and by three to four times in training with batch-integrated gradients. Area it loses by two to three orders of magnitude, and that is the row that decides racks: at the 2023 clock a rack of meshes is a thirtieth of an NVL72, and parity needs ten-gigasample converters and sixteen tiles per package. Weights do not fit in silicon, so they stream from HBM like everyone else's. Holding a phase has no clean answer. Envise's electro-optic cells are sub-millimetre with no holding current, but a weight cell only needs zero-to-one amplitude; a mesh phase needs two pi, and at CMOS voltages a depletion shifter is centimetre-class, while a ring gives two pi in ten microns but drifts with wavelength and temperature. MEMS is short and free and unproven. Then the two rows that are not physics: the digital shell, which nobody has built under a picojoule per op yet, and reliability, a million control loops and hourly recalibration on the only chip that exists. Say plainly that every number in the mesh column is projected. The next slide says what I would build.

Where light belongs: a photonic MLP engine for training and prefill backup

What to build
  • EO mesh tiles, block-multiplexed from HBM, \(10\,\mathrm{GS/s}\) converters
  • In situ gradients, integrated over a batch of \(10^4\) tokens
  • A streaming detector for attention
  • Pooled lasers, air-cooled compute
What it is
  • Training and prefill, at \(3\) to \(5\times\) the best digital efficiency
  • Not a decode engine
  • Unproven: the surrounding electronics, holding a phase without heaters, reliability
  • Unique: the chip computes its own gradients
Backup. This is the claim, and I would rather be argued with than agreed with. Build the weight-stationary part of a transformer out of block-multiplexed electro-optic mesh tiles fed from HBM, train it with the in situ gradient integrated over the batch, put attention on a streaming detector, and pool the lasers. It is a training and prefill engine. It is not a decode engine, because decode is memory-bound on every device ever built. Three things are unproven, and they are the three rows at the bottom of the last table: the shell, the phase holding, and the reliability. The one thing it has that nothing digital has is the last line: the chip computes its own gradients.

Backup for the question "is 10 gigasamples real". Four bits: yes, flash converters. Eight bits nominal: yes, at six to seven effective bits, which is what every 112G PAM4 receiver already does in 5 nanometre silicon, and ABFP-style scaling tolerates it for inference. Ten to twelve bits: no, and it does not matter, because the only thing that needs it is the gradient readout and that runs at f over M. The part that is genuinely hard is the digital shell: ten terabits per second of activations per tile is a coherent-DSP-class design, and Envise's twelve nanometre control die stalling at five hundred megahertz is the cautionary tale.

MLP versus attention: weight-stationary or not backup

The weight-stationary half
In current models the MLP is roughly half of the MACs per token, \(0.4\) to \(1.2\,\mathrm{GMACs}\) of active experts; attention takes the other half at long context. Detail in the backup.
  • The MLP's weights sit still for every token: the mesh's case
  • Attention multiplies data by data; a mesh would be rewritten every token. Stream both operands into a detector instead
  • Attention is memory-bound in decode. Mesh for the MLP, streaming unit for attention, decode left to memory
Backup. This is the slide that says where the mesh belongs. Two thirds of the weight work in a transformer is the MLP, and every token goes through it, so the weights sit still for tens of thousands of vectors. That is the mesh's case. Attention multiplies activations by activations, and there is no way to hold that in phases; you would be rewriting the mesh at terabits per second. The circuit that fits attention is the one where both operands stream into a detector. And say the bandwidth point plainly: attention is memory-bound. Every key and value is read from the cache and used once per query, so in decode the arithmetic intensity is about one MAC per byte, two orders below what it takes to keep any HBM-fed multiplier busy. Faster multiplies do nothing there, photonic or otherwise. Read the table by row. Llama 3 is the dense reference: attention is small at 8k and takes over by 128k. DeepSeek-V3.2 is the surprise: even with sparse attention, attention is a third of the MACs at 8k, because mixture of experts thinned the active MLP to 0.4 giga-MACs per token while the attention, 128 heads on a 576-wide latent, is sized for a 671-billion-parameter model; the lightning indexer is linear in context and dominates at 128k. Kimi K3 shows what linear attention does: 69 of 93 layers are Kimi Delta Attention with no context dependence, and the MLP is 1.2 giga-MACs, so attention stays under 15 percent to 128k and under half at a million. DeepSeek-V4 compresses the KV cache 10 times and lands near half at a million. The lesson for a photonic MLP engine: mixture of experts moved compute share toward attention, and only linear or compressed attention moves it back. Put the two circuits together and you cover training and prefill. Decode is a memory problem for everyone, including us.

Scaling past one mesh: tiling backup

  • Each tile is an SVD: two meshes and \(N\) attenuators
  • Error resets at every tile's ADC
  • Reload and fetch: \(\approx2.5\,\mathrm{fJ}\) per MAC at \(M=10^4\)
Backup. The question always comes: your mesh is 128 wide, my matrix is 28 thousand. The answer is tiling, and it is better than it sounds. Every tile is its own SVD, its output is digitised, so error is capped per tile rather than compounding over the whole matrix. The conversion cost per MAC is exactly what it was for one mesh. What tiling does cost is weight traffic, and that is the same rule every accelerator lives under: reuse each tile over enough vectors. At ten thousand tokens per weight per step, which is what training does, it is under three femtojoules per MAC.

In situ backpropagation and inverse design backup

The adjoint method, as in photonic inverse design
  • Adjoint method: a gradient is the interference of a forward and a backward field (Hughes, Minkov, Fan 2018)
  • Forward input, backward error, then their sum: tap power at each phase shifter reads out \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta\)
Experimental results
  • 3-layer, 4-port silicon photonic network with thermal phase shifters and grating taps imaged by an IR camera
  • Analog update: one toggle applies the gradient, with no digital subtraction and no \(O(N^2)\) readback
  • Ring and moons trained on chip: \(90\) to \(98\%\) test accuracy, tracking digital training curves
  • Simulated \(64\times64\) MNIST: \(97\%\) ideal, \(95\%\) with tap noise and I/O error

Why width is hard: light through a 128-mesh backup

  • A random unitary is not a random set of phases: Haar-distributed meshes concentrate the MZIs near cross state toward the centre (top row)
  • Initialise uniformly and light collapses onto a few paths (bottom row); the gradient landscape has the same structure
  • Every error term in the energy equation compounds over the \(2N\) stages light crosses, so width buys efficiency and costs accuracy at the same rate
  • This is why training a mesh needs the device in the loop: the parametrisation is far from the matrix it represents

Inference versus training: the energy budget backup

Digital bars: datasheet peak dense throughput over board power. Envise: \(65.5\,\mathrm{TOPS}\) at \(78\,\mathrm{W}\) electrical plus \(1.6\,\mathrm{W}\) optical (Harris, Apr 2025). Formats differ; read as orders of magnitude.

A 2025 photonic system sits about \(10\times\) above GPU peak, with \(98\%\) of its power in electronics. The optics are not the term to optimize.

Inference: one forward pass
  • Tolerates \(4\) to \(8\) bits, the analog-friendly regime
  • Weights static, so phase holding amortizes
  • Cost is dominated by conversions, not photons
Training: forward, backward, update
  • Gradients want 16-bit dynamic range, analog's weak spot
  • In situ backprop: the backward pass is one more optical pass, and the update is \(O(1)\) per phase instead of \(O(N^2)\) memory traffic
  • But each step rewrites \(N^2\) phases, so weight write energy and speed set the budget
Be careful with the chart: peak datasheet numbers versus a measured research system. The point is not the ratio, it is which term dominates. In situ training removes the memory traffic term, but it does not touch the conversion term.
