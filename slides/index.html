[0:20] Motivation in one breath. The numbers on the left are the crisis: capex above three hundred billion a year across the hyperscalers on 2025 guidance, datacenters going from about four percent of US electricity in 2023 to a projected seven to twelve percent by 2028 in the Berkeley Lab report, and campuses now sized in gigawatts. In 2022 we built a chip that trains with light, on the premise that the multiply was the expensive thing. Since then the models reached what we now call AGI-class capability and the workload changed underneath us: transformers, four-bit inference, decode that waits on memory. The point of the talk is to redo the 2022 argument against the 2026 workload, honestly, and say where light belongs.

The 2010s thesis: a programmable mesh for quantum and AI

An MZI: \(\phi\), coupler, \(\theta\), coupler. Sweep \(\theta,\phi\) and all the light goes to one port. A mesh of them is any \(N\times N\) unitary.

Quantum: the mesh is the computer
  • Linear optics, photons and detectors are universal (KLM 2001)
  • Reprogrammable 6-mode interferometer on a chip (Carolan 2015)
AI: the mesh as a matrix engine
  • Coherent nanophotonic deep learning (Shen 2017)
  • \(N^2\) MACs per pass for \(O(N)\) conversions

Well over $1B of venture funding followed the AI thesis, 2017 to 2023.

[0:50] One device, five literatures: universal linear optics after Reck and Clements, self-configuring optics after Miller, quantum photonics after Carolan, neural networks after Shen, programmable photonics after Bogaerts. The animation is the primitive: sweep the two phases of one MZI and steer all the light into one port; that is how a mesh is programmed and how it is read out. Two communities adopted the same hardware for opposite reasons. Quantum needs the mesh to be exact. AI needs it to be cheap. Keep that tension in mind.

The post-GPT workload: a transformer, and prefill versus decode

  • A token is a word piece, a vector of length \(d\) (\(8192\) for a 70B model), through \(80\) of these blocks
  • Green: weights that never change at inference. Amber: the token against every earlier one, from the KV cache
  • Training looks like prefill, with thousands of tokens per weight per step
  • The mesh fits prefill and training. Decode is a memory problem for any multiplier
[1:45] Definitions first, this is a photonics room. A token is a word piece; the model turns it into a vector of eight thousand numbers and pushes it through eighty copies of the block on the left. Green boxes are ordinary matrix-times-vector with a weight matrix that never changes during inference; that is the thing an optical mesh can hold. The amber box is attention: the new token looks back at every earlier token in the conversation, whose keys and values were computed earlier and are kept in memory, the KV cache. On the right, the two regimes. Prefill is reading the prompt: all its tokens go through at once, so each weight is fetched once and reused for every token, and the chip is busy multiplying. That is the mesh's case, and training looks the same with thousands of tokens per weight. Decode is writing the answer: one token per step, and every step reads every weight and the whole cache from memory to produce a single vector, about one multiply per byte fetched. No multiplier helps there, optical or not. Photonics was adopted in the datacenter for moving numbers between chips; the multiply stayed electronic.

Optical neural network theory: three ways to hold a matrix

\[ \mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)} = f\!\left(W^{(\ell)}\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\right):\quad \text{light does } W\mathbf{x},\ \text{electronics does } f \]

All three pay for conversions at the edge, \(O(N)\) per \(N^2\) MACs. They differ in what holds the weight: a phase, a cell, or nothing.

[2:30] Three ways to make light do a matrix. The mesh holds the weights as phases and does the multiply and the sum by interference, so it needs coherence across the whole array and one interferometer per weight. The crossbar, which is what Envise actually is, holds the weights as attenuation cells, broadcasts the input, and sums photocurrents on a wire, so there is no phase to hold but still a device per weight. Temporal mapping holds nothing: the weights arrive as pulses from memory and multiply the input at a detector, so the silicon is order N and the cost moves to memory bandwidth. All three convert at the edge, order N per N squared multiplies; that shared term is the energy story later. What differs is where the weight lives, and that decides footprint, stability, training, and what happens in decode.

In situ backpropagation: the method

1 Forward. Send \(x\), record the tap powers \(p_\eta\).
2 Backward. Send the error \(y_{\mathrm{adj}}\) from the output; record \(p_{\eta,\mathrm{adj}}\).
3 Sum. Send \(x - i\,x_{\mathrm{adj}}^{*}\); the tap power is the gradient.
\[ \underbrace{\textcolor{#3A8FB7}{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\eta} = -\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(x_\eta\,x_{\mathrm{adj},\eta}\right)}}_{\textcolor{#3A8FB7}{\text{adjoint method}}} \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\left(p_{\eta,\mathrm{sum}} - p_\eta - p_{\eta,\mathrm{adj}}\right)}_{\textcolor{#5B6B7A}{\text{digital subtraction}}} \;=\; \underbrace{\textcolor{#D9821E}{\tfrac14\left(p_{\eta,+} - p_{\eta,-}\right)}}_{\textcolor{#D9821E}{\text{experimental implementation}}} \]
Fig. 1E, Pai et al., Science 380, 398 (2023).
[3:30] Point at the three colored paths. Forward, backward, sum. The gradient is a power measurement: interference between the forward field and the back-propagated error at each phase shifter, exactly the adjoint method from inverse design, and it does not care about fabrication error because the chip is doing the differentiation. Third formula is the analog version: toggle the adjoint phase, high-pass filter, done.

  • A, B: 15 MZIs, TiN heaters, a tap at every shifter, an IR camera on a stage
  • C to F: the analog update. Toggle \(\zeta\), integrate the AC tap power, write the voltage
Fig. 2 and Fig. S1, Pai et al. (2023). Ring and moons trained on chip to \(90\) to \(98\%\).
[4:30] This is what a research demonstration looks like: the detector is a camera, the "ADC" is a microscope stage, and the phase shifters are heaters at 50 milliwatts each. Panels C to F are the part to dwell on: the analog update. Toggle the adjoint phase, the AC part of the tap power is the gradient, integrate it over the batch, write the voltage. That is the batch-gradient story on the energy slide, demonstrated on one MZI with op-amps. Every number in the energy model later replaces one of these with an integrated part. The point of the slide is that the protocol worked on a chip with real loss and real crosstalk.

Energy per operation: the 2023 model, Envise, and a 4-bit projection

2023 SM model, \(N=128\), segmented phase shifters, no DACs; Envise from the Nature 2025 SI.

4 bits: inference at \(14\,\mathrm{fJ/op}\), \(8\times\) under B200 FP4 and \(3.7\times\) under Jalapeño MXFP4 at the wall, before the shell. Jalapeño is an inference part, so it is absent from the training panel. Training: integrate the gradient over the batch; \(53\,\mathrm{fJ/op}\) at \(M=4096\), \(3.4\times\) under Cerebras FP16.

[6:15] Chart basis: Science 2023 SM Tables S1 to S4 at N equals 128 and 1 gigahertz. Envise: 78 watts plus 1.6 watts optical at 65.5 TOPS, 0.25 watts in the encode path including its weight DACs, from the Nature 2025 SI. Assumptions behind the 4-bit projection: ADC energy scales as 2 to the bits (Walden); the Nature SI's thermal-limited 4 to the bits would be 16 times more optimistic. Digital op divided by 3 is the B200 FP4 to H100 INT8 ratio at the wall. Optical power scales as 4 to the bits from shot noise, so 4-bit inference needs 256 times less light and 12-bit gradient readout needs 256 times more; that is the 3.4 picojoule per op bar, 3 picojoules of it laser. Training is not projected to 4 bits because gradients need precision; instead the gradient readout is batch-integrated, which is what the SM's analog update does: the tap current is integrated over the batch and digitised once per phase shifter per batch, so a slow 12 to 16 bit ADC at f over M is enough and the average gains half of log2 M bits, six at M equals 4096. Realistic M today is thousands of tokens per weight per device for an LLM micro-batch and millions of positions per layer for a CNN, so the SM's 16 to 256 was conservative. Two ADCs, two rates: activations at 8 bits and 1 gigasample, gradients at 12 plus bits and megasamples. Segmented phase shifters everywhere means every weight cell needs b digital lines from the control die: 65 thousand contacts at 4 bits for a 128 mesh. Baselines: B200 FP4 at 110 femtojoules per op and OpenAI's Jalapeno MXFP4 ASIC at 52, both peak over package power; Cerebras WSE-3 at 180 for FP16. The honest gap is now under four times at 4 bits before any shell is counted. Three things to say. One: the 2023 tables already predicted that conversions would dominate, and Envise confirmed it with a factor of 40 between the tensor core and the package. Two: 4-bit inference is where the optics win on paper. Three: training gets a different gift, time: integrate the gradient over the batch and precision comes from averaging, not from light or from a fast ADC. Systematic tap errors do not average, so calibration is the price.

[7:15] This slide is the discussion point. The claim is not that photonics is cold, it is that the kilowatt package and the metre of copper were both choices. Say the thermal-phase-shifter number out loud: 800 watts per mesh is what happens if you keep heaters. Then the honest part: the package power is the digital shell, and Envise measured 25 times the photonic core. [7:50] This is the slide for the skeptic in the room. Energy per op can be good and the chip still loses because cost is area, and photonics is a hundred times worse per weight. Opticore's answer is to stop storing weights in silicon at all and stream them as pulses; the price is that weights now move on every use, so they inherit the GPU's arithmetic-intensity problem and an HBM stack. The mesh can play the same game at block level if the phase shifters switch in nanoseconds and the control die can push the bits, and unlike temporal mapping it keeps a stationary device to hang a gradient loop on. Sources: docs/footprint-tdm.md.

Scaling past one mesh: tiling

  • Each tile is an SVD: two meshes and \(N\) attenuators
  • Conversions per MAC unchanged. Error resets at every tile's ADC
  • Reload and HBM fetch: \(\approx2.5\,\mathrm{fJ}\) per MAC at \(M=10^4\)
[8:15] The question always comes: your mesh is 128 wide, my matrix is 28 thousand. The answer is tiling, and it is better than it sounds. Every tile is its own SVD, its output is digitised, so error is capped per tile rather than compounding over the whole matrix. The conversion cost per MAC is exactly what it was for one mesh. What tiling does cost is weight traffic, and that is the same rule every accelerator lives under: reuse each tile over enough vectors. At ten thousand tokens per weight per step, which is what training does, it is under three femtojoules per MAC.

Why attention wants a different circuit
  • Attention multiplies activations by activations. A mesh would be rewritten every token; stream both operands into a detector instead
  • Attention is memory-bound: \(\sim1\) MAC per byte in decode
  • Mesh for the MLP, streaming unit for attention. Decode is left to memory
[8:45] This is the slide that says where the mesh belongs. Two thirds of the weight work in a transformer is the MLP, and every token goes through it, so the weights sit still for tens of thousands of vectors. That is the mesh's case. Attention multiplies activations by activations, and there is no way to hold that in phases; you would be rewriting the mesh at terabits per second. The circuit that fits attention is the one where both operands stream into a detector. And say the bandwidth point plainly: attention is memory-bound. Every key and value is read from the cache and used once per query, so in decode the arithmetic intensity is about one MAC per byte, two orders below what it takes to keep any HBM-fed multiplier busy. Faster multiplies do nothing there, photonic or otherwise. Put the two circuits together and you cover training and prefill. Decode is a memory problem for everyone, including us.

What changed after 2022?

Precision collapsed
FP8 in 2022, FP4 in 2024. Inference walked into the regime analog optics always needed.
Attention moved the bottleneck
Decode is bandwidth-bound. A faster multiply attacks the wrong term.
Inference became the product
ChatGPT, then test-time compute. Training chips are a market of a few buyers.
The ADC wall stayed put
Conversion energy improves far slower than logic, and every activation pays it twice.

Revisit the four conditions: fan-in large (still hard), precision low (now true), weights static (true for inference), data already analog (not in a datacenter).

[9:20] Two of the four flipped in our favor. The two that did not are both about where the data lives, which is the hint for the last slide.

Other applications: quantum and analog signal processing backup

Quantum photonics: exactness first
  • Meshes are the interferometers, switches, and multiplexers of linear-optical quantum computers (Carolan 2015; Xanadu and silicon platforms, Nature 2025)
  • No DAC or ADC in the data path: photons in, detector clicks out
  • Loss, extinction, and phase accuracy are what matter; accelerator calibration methods transfer directly
Analog signal processing and sensing
  • Mode sorting and unscrambling for free-space and multimode links (Miller 2013; Annoni 2017)
  • RF photonic filtering and beamforming, lidar wavefront control, optical switching
  • The signal is already light: conversions only at the boundary, weights updated at kHz by feedback
The transferable toolkit: parallel programming of feedforward meshes, power monitoring with two detectors instead of taps, and in situ gradient measurement. Each was built to make an accelerator trustworthy; each is worth more where the mesh is the signal path. Pai et al., JSTQE 2020; Nanophotonics 2023; Science 2023.

In situ backpropagation and inverse design backup

The adjoint method, as in photonic inverse design
  • Adjoint method: a gradient is the interference of a forward and a backward field (Hughes, Minkov, Fan 2018)
  • Forward input, backward error, then their sum: tap power at each phase shifter reads out \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta\)
Experimental results
  • 3-layer, 4-port silicon photonic network with thermal phase shifters and grating taps imaged by an IR camera
  • Analog update: one toggle applies the gradient, with no digital subtraction and no \(O(N^2)\) readback
  • Ring and moons trained on chip: \(90\) to \(98\%\) test accuracy, tracking digital training curves
  • Simulated \(64\times64\) MNIST: \(97\%\) ideal, \(95\%\) with tap noise and I/O error

Why width is hard: light through a 128-mesh backup

  • A random unitary is not a random set of phases: Haar-distributed meshes concentrate the MZIs near cross state toward the centre (top row)
  • Initialise uniformly and light collapses onto a few paths (bottom row); the gradient landscape has the same structure
  • Every error term in the energy equation compounds over the \(2N\) stages light crosses, so width buys efficiency and costs accuracy at the same rate
  • This is why training a mesh needs the device in the loop: the parametrisation is far from the matrix it represents
Backup. Use if asked why meshes have not scaled past 64 wide.

Inference versus training: the energy budget backup

Digital bars: datasheet peak dense throughput over board power. Envise: \(65.5\,\mathrm{TOPS}\) at \(78\,\mathrm{W}\) electrical plus \(1.6\,\mathrm{W}\) optical (Harris, Apr 2025). Formats differ; read as orders of magnitude.

A 2025 photonic system sits about \(10\times\) above GPU peak, with \(98\%\) of its power in electronics. The optics are not the term to optimize.

Inference: one forward pass
  • Tolerates \(4\) to \(8\) bits, the analog-friendly regime
  • Weights static, so phase holding amortizes
  • Cost is dominated by conversions, not photons
Training: forward, backward, update
  • Gradients want 16-bit dynamic range, analog's weak spot
  • In situ backprop: the backward pass is one more optical pass, and the update is \(O(1)\) per phase instead of \(O(N^2)\) memory traffic
  • But each step rewrites \(N^2\) phases, so weight write energy and speed set the budget
Be careful with the chart: peak datasheet numbers versus a measured research system. The point is not the ratio, it is which term dominates. In situ training removes the memory traffic term, but it does not touch the conversion term.
